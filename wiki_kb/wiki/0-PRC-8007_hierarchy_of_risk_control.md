# Hierarchy of Risk Control

The Hierarchy of Risk Control is a fundamental principle applied during the [[0-PRC-8007_job_hazard_analysis_process_steps]] to develop effective safe job procedures. It prioritizes control measures based on their effectiveness in eliminating or minimizing hazards. Controls are applied in the following order, from most effective to least effective:

1.  **Elimination or Substitution (Most Effective):**
    *   This involves finding a new way to do the job that completely eliminates the hazard, or substituting a hazardous process or substance with a less hazardous one. This is the most desirable and effective control measure.

2.  **Changing the Physical Condition:**
    *   Modifying the physical environment or conditions that create the hazard. This could involve redesigning workspaces, equipment, or processes to inherently reduce risk.

3.  **Engineering Controls:**
    *   Implementing physical changes to the workplace, equipment, or process to isolate people from the hazard or reduce the hazard itself. Examples include installing guards on machinery, using mechanical aids to reduce manual handling, or ventilation systems to remove airborne contaminants.

4.  **Administrative Controls:**
    *   Establishing safe work procedures, practices, and policies to reduce exposure to hazards. This includes measures such as reducing exposure time, rotating employees to minimize individual exposure, implementing detailed procedures, and using clear signage and warnings.

5.  **Personal Protective Equipment (PPE):**
    *   Providing workers with equipment to protect them from hazards when other controls are not feasible or sufficient. Examples include safety glasses, hard hats, gloves, and respirators. PPE is considered a lower-level control as it does not eliminate the hazard but rather protects the individual.

6.  **Additional Training (Least Effective):**
    *   Providing additional training to employees to ensure they understand the hazards and how to work safely. While essential, training alone is the least effective control measure as it relies on human behavior and may not prevent incidents if higher-level controls are absent.

By systematically applying this hierarchy, organizations can strive for the most robust and sustainable hazard control solutions.
